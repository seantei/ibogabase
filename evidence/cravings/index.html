Evidence

Cravings

LAST REVIEWED 2026-05-18 · 346 SOURCES · 0 CORRECTIONS

Withdrawal and craving findings appear in OUD studies, observational detoxification reports, and substance-use reviews.

Confidence: ModerateSource type: Study
Evidence level
early human signal by context
Sample sizes
Varies by source; each claim needs substance type, timing, and follow-up window.
Design quality
Acute symptom change is easier to observe than durable relapse prevention.
Outcomes
Withdrawal severity, cravings, time to relapse, treatment retention, and post-acute symptoms must not be merged.
Limitations
Concurrent medications, setting, expectation, follow-up loss, and substance-specific withdrawal risks can change interpretation.
Safety signals
Withdrawal physiology, dehydration, electrolytes, cardiac risk, benzodiazepines, alcohol, stimulants, opioids, and polysubstance use.
What is not proven
Craving reduction or withdrawal relief is not the same as a cure.
Best next source
OUD papers, clinical trial registries, and adverse-event literature.

Key human studies

All human craving data come from small, uncontrolled, unblinded case series and clinic cohorts with short follow-up and declared conflicts of interest. Reported craving reductions cannot be separated from concurrent acute withdrawal relief. Ibogaine is not proven to reduce drug cravings or treat substance use disorder. Mash et al., 2018Frontiers in Pharmacology Open-label case series, inpatient, with multi-dimensional craving questionnaires before/after treatment (uncontrolled, no placebo, no blinding) 191 volunteers seeking inpatient detoxification from opioids or cocaine, with medical monitoring Per the abstract, ibogaine in a safe dose range diminished opioid withdrawal symptoms and reduced heroin and cocaine cravings on multi-dimensional craving questionnaires; one-month follow-up reviewed where available. The abstract states there were no significant adverse events in the dose range studied. Up to 1 month (where data available) No control or placebo group; craving measured by self-report questionnaires; one-month follow-up only for a subset. Abstract frames prior evidence as anecdotal case reports and self-help-group testimonials. Lead author Mash is associated with commercial ibogaine development (DemeRx), a financial conflict of interest. Craving reduction is confounded with concurrent acute withdrawal relief. PMID, journal, year, DOI, design, N and outcome all confirmed against PubMed.
Malcolm et al., 2018Journal of Psychoactive Drugs Retrospective observational study using a validated craving scale (Brief Substance Craving Scale) plus COWS and SOWS at 48/24h before and 24/48h after treatment (uncontrolled, no control group, no blinding) 50 participants with opioid use disorder undergoing a week-long ibogaine detoxification protocol at a Crossroads Treatment Center clinic in Tijuana, Mexico At 48h post-administration the abstract reports 78% of patients showed no objective clinical signs of opioid withdrawal (COWS), 79% reported minimal cravings for opioids, and 68% reported subjective withdrawal symptoms in the mild range, all significantly lower than baseline. Authors note results warrant rigorous controlled trials. 48 hours post-treatment (acute only) Retrospective, no control group, very short (48h) follow-up so durability of craving reduction is unknown. PubMed MeSH confirms Retrospective Studies. Two authors (Polanco, Barsuglia) affiliated with Crossroads Treatment Center, the clinic providing the treatment, a clear conflict of interest. Acute craving drop is inseparable from withdrawal resolution. PMID, journal, year, DOI, N and outcome all confirmed against PubMed.
Brown & Alper, 2018The American Journal of Drug and Alcohol Abuse Prospective observational study (uncontrolled, no placebo); outcomes via SOWS and Addiction Severity Index Composite drug-use scores. PubMed indexes it as an Observational Study. 30 adults with DSM-IV opioid dependence (25 male, 5 female) at a clinic in Mexico; predominantly oxycodone and/or heroin users with multiple prior treatment episodes Per the abstract, SOWS withdrawal scores fell from 31.0 to 14.0 by ~76.5h post-treatment (p<0.001); at 1-month follow-up 50% (15/30) reported no opioid use in the prior 30 days; ASI Composite drug-use scores improved at all timepoints (p<0.001), with the maximal effect at 1 month that was sustained from 3-12 months at levels not reaching equivalence to the 1-month effect. 12 months (1, 3, 6, 9, 12 month timepoints) Reports withdrawal and drug-use outcomes rather than a standalone validated craving scale, but is a core OUD outcome cohort cited in the craving literature. No control group, small N, waning effect after 1 month implies relapse. Drawn from an unregulated clinic setting in Mexico. PMID, journal, year, DOI, N and outcome all confirmed against PubMed.
Noller, Frampton & Yazar-Klosinski, 2018The American Journal of Drug and Alcohol Abuse 12-month observational follow-up study (uncontrolled); ASI-Lite, BDI-II, and SOWS. PubMed indexes it as an Observational Study. 14 participants (50% female) receiving legal ibogaine treatment for opioid dependence in New Zealand from two treatment providers Per the abstract, among the 8 completers ASI-Lite drug-use composite fell significantly from baseline to 12 months (p=0.002); BDI-II depression scores fell (p<0.001); SOWS withdrawal fell acutely after treatment for all 14 participants (p=0.015). Concluded a single treatment achieved opioid cessation or sustained reduced use over 12 months. 12 months YEAR CORRECTED: researcher listed 2018; PubMed publication year is 2017 (vol 44, issue 1, pp 37-46). ONE PARTICIPANT DIED DURING TREATMENT, confirmed verbatim in the abstract, a direct fatal safety signal. Very small N (14 enrolled, only 8 completed all interviews; high attrition). No control group. Co-author Yazar-Klosinski is a full-time salaried MAPS employee per the affiliation, a conflict of interest. Reports drug-use/withdrawal rather than a dedicated craving scale. PMID, journal, DOI, N and outcome confirmed against PubMed.

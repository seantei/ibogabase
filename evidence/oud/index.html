Opioid use disorder

LAST REVIEWED 2026-05-18 · 346 SOURCES · 0 CORRECTIONS

Observational detoxification reports, follow-up studies, and registered oral Ibogaine withdrawal research.

Confidence: ModerateSource type: Study
Evidence level
early human and observational
Sample sizes
Published studies and registries vary from small cohorts to planned early-phase trial stages; each source needs its own denominator.
Design quality
Often uncontrolled, selection-biased, and setting-dependent; approved medications remain the essential comparator.
Outcomes
Withdrawal interruption, craving, relapse, retention, adverse events, and functional recovery require separate measurement.
Limitations
Durability, relapse denominator data, missing follow-up, concurrent supports, and adverse-event denominator data remain incomplete.
Safety signals
Cardiac risk, concurrent substances, withdrawal physiology, electrolyte issues, psychiatric status, and medication interactions.
What is not proven
Ibogaine is not proven to cure opioid addiction.
Best next source
ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05029401 and peer-reviewed OUD detoxification outcome papers.

Key human studies

These are the main published human studies of ibogaine for opioid use disorder. All are small and uncontrolled or open-label — there is no published randomized, placebo-controlled trial. The figures below describe these specific groups of patients; they do not establish that ibogaine is safe or effective, and they cannot be compared directly to approved treatments such as methadone or buprenorphine. Two of these studies record a death. Open each source to read its full methods and limitations.

Study Design Participants Main reported outcome Follow-up Notes & limitations
Brown & Alper, 2018Am J Drug Alcohol Abuse Prospective observational; single group, no control 30 adults with opioid dependence (Mexico) Withdrawal scores fell about 55% within ~3 days. At 1 month, 15 of 30 (50%) reported no opioid use in the prior 30 days; drug-use gains persisted at 3–12 months but below the 1-month peak. 12 months No comparison group; self-selected clinic patients; self-reported outcomes.
Noller et al., 2018Am J Drug Alcohol Abuse Observational; single group, no control 14 adults with opioid dependence (New Zealand) Significant reductions in addiction-severity (drug use) and depression scores over 12 months; withdrawal scores fell immediately after treatment. 12 months (8 of 14 completed all interviews) One participant died during treatment. Very small sample; partial follow-up; an author was funded by MAPS (declared conflict of interest).
Knuijver et al., 2022Addiction Open-label safety study; single group, hospital-monitored 14 adults with opioid use disorder (Netherlands) A single weight-based dose caused major heart-rhythm risk: average peak QTc prolongation of 95 ms, and half of patients exceeded a QTc of 500 ms (a recognized danger threshold). All patients had severe temporary loss of coordination. No torsades de pointes occurred under monitoring. At least 24 hours, in hospital Designed to measure cardiac and cerebellar safety, not effectiveness. Directly supports the serious cardiac-risk warning.
Alper et al., 1999Am J Addict Retrospective case series; non-medical settings 33 people treated for heroin withdrawal Withdrawal resolved within 24 hours in 25 of 33, sustained across 72 hours of observation. 72 hours One fatality (possible concurrent heroin use). No medical monitoring; earliest systematic case series.

Study details verified against PubMed. These figures describe individual studies and are not evidence of general safety or effectiveness. Ibogaine remains Schedule I in the U.S., is not an approved treatment, and carries serious, documented cardiac risks that require qualified medical supervision.

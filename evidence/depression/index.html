Evidence

Depression and mood symptoms

LAST REVIEWED 2026-05-18 · 346 SOURCES · 0 CORRECTIONS

Mood improvements appear in some Ibogaine-related observational and veteran studies, usually as secondary outcomes.

Confidence: ModerateSource type: Study
Evidence level
secondary outcomes and small reports
Sample sizes
Small cohorts and case-level reports dominate the public literature.
Design quality
Depression-specific controlled Ibogaine evidence remains limited.
Outcomes
Depression scale changes, quality of life, suicidality, sleep, and substance-use outcomes must be separated.
Limitations
Expectation effects, concurrent treatment, post-acute recovery, integration, and regression to the mean can affect mood measures.
Safety signals
Suicidality, bipolar disorder, psychosis risk, serotonergic medications, sleep disruption, and cardiac risk.
What is not proven
Ibogaine is not proven as a depression treatment.
Best next source
2026 psychiatric scoping review and condition-specific controlled trials if they appear.

Key human studies

No standalone human trial of ibogaine for depression exists. All human mood data are secondary outcomes inside small, uncontrolled addiction or TBI cohorts, or qualitative interview themes. Ibogaine and noribogaine are not proven to treat depression or improve mood. Open each source to read its full methods and limitations.

Study Design Participants Main reported outcome Follow-up Notes & limitations
Cherian et al., 2024Nature Medicine (Nat Med) Prospective open-label, single-arm observational study (no control, no blinding); magnesium co-administered (MISTIC protocol); ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04313712 30 male Special Operations Forces veterans with predominantly mild traumatic brain injury; depression was a secondary outcome measured by the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) Significant improvement in depression at 1 month after a single treatment (P < 0.001, Cohen's d = 2.80); also improvements in PTSD (d = 2.54) and anxiety (d = 2.13) at 1 month. Functioning (WHODAS) was the primary outcome (improved immediately, d = 0.74, and at 1 month, d = 2.20). Immediately post-treatment (primary) and 1 month post-treatment (secondary); no longer-term controlled follow-up PMID confirmed in PubMed; journal/year/design/N/outcome match the abstract exactly. Open-label, no placebo/control arm, small all-male self-selected sample; depression only a secondary outcome. Three authors are affiliated with Ambio Life Sciences (ibogaine treatment provider) — commercial conflict of interest. Abstract reports no unexpected or serious adverse events, but ibogaine carries documented cardiotoxicity (fatal arrhythmia) that magnesium was used to attempt to mitigate. Effect sizes are very large for an uncontrolled design; expectancy/placebo effects uncontrolled. Authors themselves state controlled trials are needed.
Noller et al., 2018The American Journal of Drug and Alcohol Abuse (Am J Drug Alcohol Abuse) Observational 12-month follow-up study, single-arm, no control; single ibogaine treatment by one of two providers in New Zealand 14 opioid-dependent adults (50% female); depression a secondary outcome measured by Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II); primary outcome was addiction severity (ASI-Lite) Among the 8 participants completing all interviews, BDI-II scores fell significantly from baseline to 12-month follow-up (P < 0.001); ASI-Lite drug-use composite score also fell significantly (p = 0.002). SOWS withdrawal scores fell acutely in all 14 (p = 0.015). 12 months (depression analysis limited to the 8 completers) PMID confirmed in PubMed; all fields match the abstract exactly. Very small sample, high attrition (8 of 14 completed all interviews), no control group or blinding; depression a secondary endpoint. One enrolled patient DIED during treatment — a direct safety signal stated in the abstract. Co-author Yazar-Klosinski is a salaried MAPS employee (advocacy-funding conflict of interest, explicitly noted in the author affiliation). Confounded by concurrent reduction in opioid use, so mood change cannot be attributed to a direct antidepressant effect.
Mash et al., 2000Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences (Ann N Y Acad Sci) Open-label inpatient detoxification study (pharmacokinetics, safety and preliminary efficacy); single-arm, no control. PubMed indexes the article type as 'Clinical Trial,' but the abstract describes uncontrolled preliminary efficacy measures with no comparison arm. Cocaine- and heroin-dependent persons undergoing inpatient ibogaine detoxification; depressive symptoms assessed by self-report questionnaire (secondary measure) Self-reported depressive symptoms were significantly lower after ibogaine treatment and at 30 days after program discharge; craving for cocaine and heroin also significantly decreased. Through 30 days after program discharge PMID confirmed in PubMed; journal/year/DOI match. Exact sample size and statistical detail for the depression measure are NOT stated in the abstract (reported only qualitatively as 'significantly lower') — N left null accordingly. No control group or blinding; depression a secondary self-report measure confounded by acute withdrawal/detox. Authors hypothesize aftereffects relate to the metabolite noribogaine. No exact N or follow-up retention figures available from the abstract.
Walker et al., 2026Journal of Psychoactive Drugs (J Psychoactive Drugs) Qualitative collective case study; semi-structured interviews with inductive thematic analysis (no quantitative depression scale, no control) 10 opioid-dependent individuals in New Zealand who used legally prescribed ibogaine for opioid detoxification One of seven inductive themes was 'effects of treatment on depression and anxiety'; participants associated treatment with improved mood and reduced anxiety, though relapse occurred in some cases. No quantitative depression outcome was measured. Retrospective interviews about post-treatment experience; no defined quantitative follow-up window PMID confirmed in PubMed; design/population/outcome match the abstract. Qualitative/self-reported lived-experience data only — not a measured depression outcome; subject to recall and selection bias (recruited through ibogaine networks/OST services/snowball sampling). Useful as patient-experience context, not as efficacy evidence. Emphasizes medical screening and post-treatment psychosocial support as critical to positive outcomes.

Study details verified against PubMed. These figures describe individual studies and are not evidence of general safety or effectiveness. Ibogaine remains Schedule I in the U.S., is not an approved treatment, and carries serious, documented cardiac risks that require qualified medical supervision.

Evidence

PTSD and trauma symptoms

LAST REVIEWED 2026-05-18 · 346 SOURCES · 0 CORRECTIONS

PTSD-related signals often appear within veteran or substance-use populations rather than standalone PTSD trials.

Confidence: ModerateSource type: Study
Evidence level
secondary outcomes and observational
Sample sizes
Currently tied mainly to small observational cohorts and secondary analyses.
Design quality
Controlled studies with clear PTSD endpoints and adequate follow-up remain limited.
Outcomes
PTSD symptom scales, sleep, functioning, substance use, depression, and narrative meaning are not the same outcome.
Limitations
Confounding, expectation effects, integration/support differences, psychiatric exclusions, and concurrent interventions.
Safety signals
Psychiatric destabilization, suicidality screening, medications, acute monitoring, and post-session support.
What is not proven
Ibogaine is not proven as a PTSD treatment.
Best next source
Veteran TBI studies and updated psychiatric scoping reviews.

Key human studies

There is no standalone trial of ibogaine for PTSD. Every finding below is a secondary or correlational outcome inside small, single-arm, open-label or retrospective studies of male military veterans — and half of these cohorts received ibogaine combined with 5-MeO-DMT, so PTSD effects cannot be attributed to ibogaine alone. Three of the rows reuse the same 30-person cohort. Ibogaine is not proven to treat PTSD or trauma symptoms. Open each source to read its full methods and limitations.

Cherian et al., 2024 (MISTIC)Nature Medicine Prospective, single-arm, open-label observational study (no control group, no blinding). ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04313712. Magnesium-ibogaine (MISTIC protocol) provided together with complementary treatment modalities. 30 male Special Operations Forces veterans with predominantly mild traumatic brain injury; PTSD was a secondary outcome. PTSD was a SECONDARY outcome. Significant improvement on the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 at 1 month after treatment (P<0.001, Cohen's d=2.54). Primary outcome was WHODAS disability (improved immediately, d=0.74, and at 1 month, d=2.20). Depression (MADRS, d=2.80) and anxiety (HAM-A, d=2.13) also improved at 1 month. 1 month (PTSD assessed at this secondary timepoint); primary WHODAS outcome assessed immediately post-treatment. Open-label, uncontrolled, all-male SOF sample limits generalizability and causal inference. Authors report no unexpected or serious adverse events; abstract explicitly notes ibogaine's known association with fatal cardiac arrhythmia, with magnesium co-administration used to mitigate cardiac risk. Three co-authors affiliated with Ambio Life Sciences, an ibogaine treatment provider (Vancouver) - confirmed conflict of interest. Verified against PubMed abstract: journal, 2024 publication, vol 30(2):373-381, n=30, design, and all effect sizes match.
Brown et al., 2025Journal of Affective Disorders Secondary mediation/association analysis of the same 30-person MISTIC cohort (linear mixed models; Mystical Experience Questionnaire MEQ30 and resting-state EEG). Not an independent sample. Same 30 male veterans with traumatic brain injury from repeated blast/combat exposure who underwent magnesium-ibogaine therapy. Greater intensity of mystical experience during treatment was associated with larger PTSD reductions immediately (time-by-MEQ30 interaction B=-5.89, p<0.001) and at 1 month (B=-4.45, p=0.007), and with decreased peak alpha frequency at 1 month (B=-0.38, p=0.006). Correlational/mediational only. Immediately post-treatment and 1 month post-treatment. Same 30 subjects as Cherian 2024 - adds no new participants and inherits all design limitations (open-label, uncontrolled, all-male). Association/mediation analysis cannot establish that ibogaine causes PTSD improvement. Stanford/VA author group overlapping with the primary MISTIC report. Verified against PubMed abstract: journal vol 395(Pt B):120722, 2025, n=30, all B values and p-values match exactly.
Sridhar et al., 2026Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging Secondary multimodal neuroimaging analysis (arterial spin labeling and BOLD fMRI) of the same 30-person MISTIC cohort, before/immediately after treatment and at 1-month. Not an independent sample. Same 30 male SOF veterans who received ibogaine treatment for traumatic brain injury sequelae. Exploratory whole-brain imaging study, not a PTSD efficacy endpoint. Treatment-induced regional cerebral blood flow changes in the left insula and left anterior cingulate cortex correlated significantly with improvements in TBI-related disability; gradual rCBF increases in cortical, limbic, and striatal subregions plus widespread functional-connectivity changes reported. Immediately post-treatment and at 1 month. Hypothesis-generating, uncontrolled imaging of the same 30-subject open-label cohort. No PTSD-specific clinical efficacy claim; included only as mechanistic context within the trauma cohort. Authors explicitly call for larger controlled neuroimaging studies. Verified against PubMed abstract: journal, 2026, n=30, left insula/left ACC rCBF finding all match.
Davis et al., 2020Chronic Stress (Thousand Oaks) Retrospective, cross-sectional survey of clinic alumni (no control group, no blinding, no randomization); before-vs-after recall of a 30-day window. 51 of 65 eligible US Special Operations Forces veterans who completed an ibogaine + 5-MeO-DMT program in Mexico, 2017-2019 (mean age 40; 96% male; 92% Caucasian/White; 96% OEF/OIF service). Very large retrospective pre-post reductions in PTSD symptoms (p<0.001, d=-3.6), alongside suicidal ideation (d=-1.9), cognitive impairment (d=-2.8), depression (d=-3.7) and anxiety (d=-3.1). Increases in retrospective psychological flexibility (d=2.9) correlated strongly with symptom reductions. Retrospective single survey; recall of 30 days before vs 30 days after treatment (no prospective follow-up). Ibogaine was co-administered with 5-MeO-DMT, so PTSD effects cannot be attributed to ibogaine specifically. Retrospective self-report design highly vulnerable to recall and selection bias (only program completers surveyed). One author affiliated with the Terra Incognita Project. Authors describe findings as preliminary. CORRECTION vs researcher row: 92% are Caucasian/White (the 96% figure cited by the researcher is the OEF/OIF service rate, not the White proportion). Verified against PubMed: journal vol 4, 2020, n=51, PTSD d=-3.6 match.
Davis et al., 2023The American Journal of Drug and Alcohol Abuse Prospective, single-arm, open-label clinical-program evaluation (no control, no blinding); online surveys at four timepoints (baseline, 1, 3, 6 months). 86 trauma-exposed male SOF veterans in an ibogaine + 5-MeO-DMT program in Mexico (mean age 42.88; 100% male; 87.2% White; 89.5% non-Hispanic). Significant self-reported PTSD symptom improvement baseline to 1 month (p<0.001, d=0.414), with smaller-to-moderate gains in depression (d=0.275), anxiety (d=0.276), insomnia severity (d=0.351), post-concussive symptoms (d=0.389), satisfaction with life (d=0.371), psychological flexibility (d=0.313) and cognitive functioning (d=0.265); signal of durability to 6 months. 1, 3 and 6 months post-treatment. Combined ibogaine + 5-MeO-DMT - PTSD effect not attributable to ibogaine alone. Self-report outcomes, no control arm, treatment-seeking all-male SOF sample. Author affiliations with the Center for Psychedelic Drug Research and Education (Ohio State) and Johns Hopkins; program-evaluation data from a private clinic in Mexico. Authors state controlled research is warranted. Verified against PubMed: journal vol 49(5):587-596, 2023, n=86, PTSD d=0.414 match.
Armstrong et al., 2023Military Psychology Prospective, single-arm, open-label clinical-program evaluation (no control, no blinding) of co-occurring risky alcohol use and PTSD symptoms; assessments at pre-treatment, 1, 3, 6 months. Subset of 45 of 86 SOF veterans meeting risky-alcohol-use criteria at baseline in an ibogaine + 5-MeO-DMT program in Mexico (mean age 44; 100% male; 91% White). PTSD was a between-group correlate. Primary focus was alcohol use, which fell significantly from pre-treatment through 6 months (partial eta squared=0.617). Alcohol-use responders (abstinent/non-risky) vs non-responders differed significantly and very largely in PTSD symptom change (p<0.01, d=-3.26) and cognitive functioning change (p<0.01, d=-0.99). 1, 3 and 6 months post-treatment. Same Mission Within Mexico cohort (overlaps Davis 2023); ibogaine combined with 5-MeO-DMT, so ibogaine-specific PTSD effect is not isolable. Open-label, uncontrolled, all-male. PTSD is a between-group correlate of alcohol response rather than a controlled efficacy endpoint. Co-author Polanco affiliated with The Mission Within (the treating clinic) - confirmed clinic conflict of interest. PubMed lists publication year 2023, journal Military Psychology vol 36(2):184-191; DOI carries a 2022 stem. Verified: n=45 of 86, PTSD d=-3.26 match.

Study details verified against PubMed. These figures describe individual studies and are not evidence of general safety or effectiveness. Ibogaine remains Schedule I in the U.S., is not an approved treatment, and carries serious, documented cardiac risks that require qualified medical supervision.

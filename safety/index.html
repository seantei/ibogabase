Safety

Safety desk

LAST REVIEWED 2026-05-18 · 346 SOURCES · 0 CORRECTIONS

Safety is the first clinical topic, not the last paragraph. Ibogaine coverage has to foreground QT/QTc, rhythm risk, medication interactions, exclusions, adverse events, and emergency readiness before benefit language.

Ibogaine is associated with serious cardiac and interaction risks. Published research and medical discussion put screening, monitoring, exclusion criteria, medication review, and emergency-response capacity ahead of any benefit.

What the published fatality record shows

The most systematic count is a forensic review of 19 deaths between 1990 and 2008 that occurred within 1.5 to 76 hours of taking ibogaine outside West Central Africa (Alper et al., 2012, J Forensic Sci). In 12 of the 14 cases with adequate post-mortem data, pre-existing medical conditions — mainly cardiovascular — and/or other substances taken alongside ibogaine explained or contributed to the death. A later toxicology review counted 27 reported fatalities and, importantly, described case reports of dangerous heart rhythms in people with no known pre-existing cardiac condition (Litjens & Brunt, 2016, Clin Toxicol).

The denominator is unknown. Nobody knows how many people take ibogaine, so these counts cannot be turned into a death rate — and they almost certainly undercount. What the record supports saying is narrower and more useful: deaths cluster around pre-existing heart disease, co-used substances, and unsupervised settings — and screening does not remove the risk, because serious rhythm events have also occurred in people without known heart problems.

Why the heart, and why days later

Ibogaine is converted by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 into its long-lived active metabolite noribogaine, which stays at meaningful blood levels for days after ibogaine itself has cleared (Litjens & Brunt, 2016). Both molecules block the heart’s hERG potassium channel, which slows the electrical “reset” (repolarization) of each heartbeat — measured on an ECG as a longer QT interval. A prolonged QT can tip into torsades de pointes, a ventricular arrhythmia that can cause sudden death. This was shown directly in human heart cells, and because noribogaine is just as active and lasts far longer, it explains the clinically observed pattern of cardiac events occurring days after ingestion, not only during the acute experience (Rubi et al., 2017, Cardiovasc Toxicol).

CYP2D6 activity varies genetically from person to person. In a controlled pharmacokinetic study, people with reduced CYP2D6 activity were exposed to roughly twice the active drug load from the same intake (Glue et al., 2015, J Clin Pharmacol) — one reason the same amount can be far more dangerous for one person than another, unpredictably without genotyping. Common medications that inhibit CYP2D6 (including some antidepressants) can produce the same effect.

Interactions and research-setting exclusions

Methadone deserves specific mention. It is the most widely used opioid-maintenance medication and is itself QT-prolonging — so combining it with ibogaine stacks two QT-prolonging drugs in exactly the population most likely to seek ibogaine. The Dutch safety study would not dose patients on methadone: participants were converted to morphine first, and even then half of them exceeded a QTc of 500 ms (Knuijver et al., 2022, Addiction). The noribogaine trial in methadone-maintained patients likewise switched them to morphine before dosing and still measured a dose-dependent QTc increase (Glue et al., 2016, Clin Pharmacol Drug Dev).

Research protocols and published safety discussions commonly exclude people with: existing QT prolongation or congenital long-QT syndrome; structural or ischemic heart disease; significant electrolyte abnormalities (potassium, magnesium); hepatic impairment (the liver clears both molecules); pregnancy; and concurrent use of QT-prolonging or CYP2D6-inhibiting medications, stimulants, alcohol, or benzodiazepine withdrawal states. This is a description of how researchers manage risk — it is not a screening checklist, and passing it does not make ibogaine safe. Serious rhythm events have occurred in people with none of these factors.
